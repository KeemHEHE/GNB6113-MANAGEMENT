## Ch.1 - Managers and the Workplace (textbook Ch.1)

- Manager: coordinates/oversees others' work so org goals are accomplished.
- Levels: First-line (manage non-managerial employees) -> Middle (manage first-line) -> Top (org-wide decisions).
  - First-line example titles: supervisor, shift manager, district manager, department manager, office manager.
  - Middle example titles: regional manager, project leader, store manager, division manager.
  - Top example titles: executive VP, president, managing director, COO, CEO.
- Holacracy: self-management structure, no formal managers, roles/teams distribute decisions (example: Mercedes-Benz IT unit).
- Organization = distinct purpose + deliberate structure + people.
- Why managers are important: organizations need managerial skills/abilities more than ever; managers are critical to getting things done; managers make a difference in org performance.
- Efficiency = doing things right (means, resource usage, low waste). Effectiveness = doing the right things (ends, goal attainment).
- 4 Management Functions: Planning -> Organizing -> Leading -> Controlling (P-O-L-C).
- Mintzberg's 10 Managerial Roles (3 groups):
  - Interpersonal: Figurehead, Leader, Liaison
  - Informational: Monitor, Disseminator, Spokesperson
  - Decisional: Entrepreneur, Disturbance Handler, Resource Allocator, Negotiator
- 3 Management Skills: Technical (most important - first-line), Interpersonal (equally important all levels), Conceptual (most important - top managers).
- Universality of management: needed in all org sizes, types, levels, areas.
- Trends: remote/hybrid work (hybrid = part remote, part on-site); digital transformation - rethinking tech/people/processes; AI = hardware/software systems that make decisions and carry out actions on their own without additional human input; gig economy = tech-enabled growth of freelance work.
- Employee well-being: a person's judgment that their life is positive, includes mental + physical health; management's DEI approach impacts it.
- The reality of work: you'll either manage or be managed; studying management gives insight into how bosses/organizations function, even for non-managers.

## Ch.2 - External Environment & Organizational Culture (textbook Ch.4)

- Omnipotent view: managers directly responsible for success/failure.
- Symbolic view: external forces largely drive success/failure - manager control is limited.
- Managerial discretion constrained by: external (environment) + internal (culture).
- Environmental uncertainty = degree of change (stable/dynamic) x degree of complexity (simple/complex). 4 cells (Exhibit 4-2):
  - Cell 1: stable + simple = lowest uncertainty (few, similar, unchanging components).
  - Cell 2: dynamic + simple = few components but continually changing.
  - Cell 3: stable + complex = many dissimilar but unchanging components, high need for sophisticated knowledge.
  - Cell 4: dynamic + complex = highest uncertainty (many, dissimilar, continually changing components).
- General environment components: Economic, Demographic, Political/Legal, Sociocultural, Technological, Global.
- Specific environment (directly relevant to goals): Suppliers, Customers, Competitors, Government, Investors, Special interest groups.
- Organizational culture: shared values/principles/traditions shaping member behavior.
- 6 Culture Dimensions: Adaptability, Attention to detail, Outcome orientation, People orientation, Team orientation, Integrity.
- Contrasting cultures example (Exhibit 4-5): Weak/rigid culture = risk-taking & creativity discouraged, close supervision, work designed around individuals. Strong/adaptive culture = risk-taking & innovation rewarded, management trusts employees, work designed around teams.
- Strong vs. weak culture: strong = values widely shared, consistent messaging, employees identify strongly, strong shared-values-to-behavior link; weak = values limited to a few (usually top mgmt), contradictory messaging, little employee identification.
- Culture origin/maintenance: Founders' philosophy -> Selection criteria -> Top management + Socialization -> Culture.
- How culture is sustained: Stories, Rituals, Material artifacts/symbols, Language.
- Remote work and culture: most culture-building knowledge assumes physical proximity; remote workers have less interaction, making culture harder to build; managers must be more explicit about articulating cultural values remotely.
- How culture affects managerial decisions (Exhibit 4-8, all 4 functions):
  - Planning: risk level in plans, individual vs. team plan development, degree of environmental scanning.
  - Organizing: employee autonomy, individual vs. team tasks, degree of inter-department interaction.
  - Leading: concern for employee job satisfaction, appropriate leadership styles, whether disagreements are eliminated.
  - Controlling: external controls vs. employee self-control, performance evaluation criteria, consequences of exceeding budget.

## Ch.3 - Foundations of Planning (textbook Ch.8)

- Planning: setting goals, establishing strategies, developing plans to coordinate work.
- Formal planning: has specific, time-oriented goals that are written and shared.
- Why plan: direction, reduces uncertainty, minimizes waste/redundancy, sets standards for controlling.
- Planning and performance: formal planning is associated with positive financial results; quality of planning/implementation matters more than the extent of planning; external factors can reduce planning's impact on performance; the planning-performance link is influenced by the planning time frame.
- Types of Plans (5 dimensions, Exhibit 8-1): Strategic/Operational (breadth), Long-term(3+yrs)/Short-term(<=1yr) (time frame), Directional/Specific (specificity), Single-use/Standing (frequency), Static/Scenario (conditions).
  - Long-term = beyond 3 years; short-term = 1 year or less.
  - Specific plans = clearly defined, no room for interpretation. Directional plans = flexible, general guidelines.
  - Single-use = one-time plan for a unique situation. Standing = ongoing plan for repeated activities.
  - Static plans = assume environment/internal conditions stay unchanged. Scenario plans = make assumptions about the future and how the company will respond to a "scenario" (a consistent view of the likely future).
- 3 Contingency factors in planning: level in organization, degree of environmental uncertainty, length of future commitments.
  - Top execs -> more strategic planning; first-line -> more operational planning (visualized as a hierarchy: strategic planning narrows going down, operational planning widens).
- Objectives = Goals (desired outcomes for individuals/groups/whole org; provide direction, measure progress). Stated (official, public-facing) vs. Real (actually pursued) objectives.
- Traditional objective-setting: top-down cascade - risk of distortion as it flows down (Exhibit 8-4 example: "improve performance" at top can become "don't worry about quality, just work fast" by the time it reaches individual employees).
- MBO (Management by Objectives): mutually agreed-upon goals used to evaluate employee performance; objectives cascade down and are translated into operational objectives at each level (Exhibit 8-5: overall org -> divisional -> departmental -> individual objectives).
- Does MBO work?: effectiveness is complex to assess; holding ability/goal-acceptance constant, more difficult goals -> higher performance; BUT if a goal is perceived as impossible (not just challenging), desire to achieve it decreases and likelihood of abandoning it increases.
- SMART objectives: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
- Environmental scanning: screening for emerging trends (Exhibit 8-6 - general environment sources: political=lobbyists, economic=Fed minutes/financial news, social=social media, technological=patent reports; specific environment sources: industry=business media, competitors=websites, suppliers=vendors, customers=surveys/focus groups). Competitive intelligence: gathering info on competitors to anticipate (not just react to) their moves.
- AI in planning: a 2024-era survey found 75% of 1,500 CEOs believe failing to integrate AI into management risks going out of business; AI helps planning by analyzing large data volumes and spotting patterns.

## Ch.4 - Making Decisions (textbook Ch.6 - standard content, no slides provided)

- Decision-making process (8 steps): Identify problem -> Identify decision criteria -> Weight criteria -> Develop alternatives -> Analyze alternatives -> Select alternative -> Implement -> Evaluate effectiveness.
- Decision types:
  - Programmed (routine, structured problems, use existing procedures - e.g., reorder stock)
  - Non-programmed (unique, unstructured problems, require custom solutions - e.g., entering a new market)
- Decision-making conditions:
  - Certainty: outcomes known.
  - Risk: outcomes estimable via probabilities.
  - Uncertainty: outcomes/probabilities unknown - rely on intuition/judgment.
- Decision-making approaches:
  - Rationality: fully rational, objective, logical choice maximizing value.
  - Bounded rationality: decisions constrained by limited info-processing ability - managers "satisfice" (accept good-enough) rather than "maximize."
  - Intuition: experience-based, unconscious judgment.
- Common decision-making biases/errors: overconfidence, immediate gratification (favoring quick payoffs over long-term gains), anchoring (fixating on initial info), selective perception (filtering info based on own views), confirmation bias (seeking info that reaffirms past choices), framing bias (highlighting certain aspects while downplaying others), availability bias (overweighting recent/vivid events), representation bias (drawing false analogies), randomness bias (creating meaning from random events), sunk costs error (fixating on past costs, not future consequences), self-serving bias (taking credit for success, blaming failure on externals), hindsight bias (believing an outcome was predictable only after it's known).
- Escalation of commitment: increasing commitment to a failing decision despite negative info, often to avoid admitting the original decision was wrong.
- Decision-making styles: based on 2 dimensions - way of thinking (linear/rational vs. nonlinear/intuitive) and tolerance for ambiguity (low vs. high). Produces 4 styles: Directive, Analytical, Conceptual, Behavioral.
- Group decision-making:
  - Pros: more complete info/knowledge, more diverse viewpoints, higher quality decisions, greater acceptance of the solution.
  - Cons: time-consuming, can be dominated by a minority (one person/subgroup), pressure to conform (groupthink), ambiguous responsibility for the outcome.
- Group decision-making techniques: brainstorming (generate alternatives without criticism), nominal group technique (members work independently before discussing), electronic meetings (computer-aided, more anonymous/faster).

## Ch.5 - Managing Strategy (textbook Ch.7 - standard content; this is your CASE STUDY chapter)

- Strategic management: what managers do to develop the organization's strategies.
- Why it matters: affects org performance, helps managers cope with uncertainty, ties diverse org units toward common goals.
- Strategic Management Process (7 steps - likely central to the case study):
  1. Identify current mission, goals, strategies
  2. External analysis (opportunities & threats - see Ch.2 environment concepts)
  3. Internal analysis (strengths & weaknesses - resources, capabilities, core competencies)
  4. SWOT analysis (combine 2+3)
  5. Formulate strategies
  6. Implement strategies
  7. Evaluate results
- 3 levels of strategy:
  - Corporate-level: what businesses to be in overall (growth, stability, renewal strategies).
  - Business-level (competitive): how to compete in each business/market.
  - Functional-level: how to support business strategy at department level (marketing, finance, HR, etc.).
- Porter's Competitive Strategies (business-level - high-yield for case study application):
  - Cost leadership: lowest costs in industry.
  - Differentiation: unique products/services valued by customers.
  - Focus: cost or differentiation advantage in a narrow segment.
  - Being "stuck in the middle" (no clear strategy) = weak position.
- Corporate strategy options:
  - Growth: expand via new markets/products, concentration, vertical/horizontal integration, diversification, or M&A.
  - Stability: maintain the status quo during uncertain times or after a growth period.
  - Renewal: retrenchment (short-term fix for minor performance issues) or turnaround (bigger fix for serious/persistent decline).
- BCG Matrix (a common corporate-level portfolio tool, often paired with strategy chapters): plots business units by market growth rate vs. market share into Stars (high/high), Cash Cows (low growth/high share), Question Marks (high growth/low share), Dogs (low/low).
- Strategic leadership: top managers' ability to understand the complexities of the org and its environment and lead strategic change.
- Strategic flexibility: ability to recognize major external changes, quickly commit resources, and recognize when a strategic decision was a mistake.
- Case-study tip: expect a company scenario - practice identifying its strategy type, SWOT factors, and whether its strategy fits its environment/culture (ties back to Ch.1-2 concepts).
